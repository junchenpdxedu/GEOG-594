Part 1

Getting PRISM data

What PRISM is, how to download the normals, and how to bring them into ArcGIS Pro

A rainfall surface built by experts

Key idea

PRISM is a gridded climate dataset made by the PRISM Climate Group at Oregon State University. It is an interpolated precipitation surface you can download instead of building your own.

Why use it?

In Lecture 5a you built surfaces from a few points. PRISM does the same job for the whole country, using thousands of stations and a digital elevation model, so it handles mountains far better than IDW or spline.

Rainfall vs. precipitation?

Precipitation is all water falling from the sky: rain, snow, sleet and hail. Rainfall is only the liquid part. PRISM grids are precipitation, so in snowy mountains they include snow water.

Read the map

The wettest areas are the Pacific Northwest coast and the Southeast; the driest are the desert Southwest and Great Basin.

Download PRISM precipitation normals

Key idea

Download the 800 m normals for 1991–2020 from the PRISM website, choosing precipitation as the variable.

Website

prism.oregonstate.edu, then 30-Year Normals

Choose

  1. Spatial resolution: 800 m
  2. Climate variable: precipitation
  3. Temporal period: annual values (or a month)
  4. Download the data, then extract the zip file to a folder

What is a “normal”?

A 30-year average. The current normals cover 1991–2020, so a normal is a long-term baseline, not one year’s rainfall.

Add the raster to ArcGIS Pro

Key idea

The uncompressed file is a raster ArcGIS Pro can read directly. Add it like any other data.

1. Check the format

The website lists the uncompressed format of each download. The slides’ example used an ASCII grid (.asc): a plain-text raster that ArcGIS Pro reads as a raster.

2. Add data

In ArcGIS Pro, click Add Data, navigate to your extracted folder, and select the raster file.

3. Look before you analyze

Before running any tool, open the metadata that came in the zip file (next slide). It tells you what you just loaded.

Troubleshooting: read the metadata!

Key idea

Most PRISM problems come from not reading the metadata. Answer two questions before you start.

What projection is the dataset in?

PRISM grids come in geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude). Distances and areas in degrees are not meaningful, so you will project the raster first.

What units are the raster cells?

Precipitation cell values are in millimeters. If your project uses inches, you must convert the values.

Why it matters

A raster in the wrong projection will not line up with your watershed layers, and a value in the wrong units makes every runoff or recharge number wrong.

Q1 A PRISM grid for a Cascade Range watershed shows 3,000 mm of annual “precipitation”. Why is that not the same as 3,000 mm of rainfall?

Precipitation includes snow (as snow water equivalent) as well as rain. At high elevation much of the 3,000 mm falls as snow.

Q2 What two questions should the metadata answer before you analyze a PRISM raster?

What projection is it in, and what units are the cell values?

Part 2

Preparing PRISM for analysis

Project, clip, resample and convert units, and how to combine steps

Pre-processing overview

Key idea

Four steps turn a national PRISM grid into a raster that matches your study area and your other data.

1. Project Raster

Project to your working projection, the same one as your other layers.

2. Extract by Mask

“Clip” the raster to your study area, for example a watershed polygon.

3. Resample

Match the cell size of your other raster data, such as a DEM.

4. Convert units

Convert the default millimeters to your desired units. For inches:

inches = mm ÷ 25.4

Use Raster Calculator.

Combine steps: clip and project on the fly

Key idea

You can project and clip in one step by exporting the raster with the map’s current extent and coordinate system.

Can you project, clip and resample in one step?

Partly. Exporting a raster can apply the extent and the coordinate system at the same time, and you can set an output cell size. Unit conversion is still a separate Raster Calculator step.

How

Right-click the precipitation layer › Data › Export Raster

  • Extent / clipping: the current display extent (or your study-area layer)
  • Coordinate system: the current map

Older ArcMap slides call these “Data Frame (Current)” and “Spatial Reference (Current)”.

What can you do with PRISM data?

Key idea

Once it is prepared, a PRISM grid is the precipitation input for many hydrologic analyses.

Runoff models

Precipitation is the first input to rainfall–runoff and water balance models (Lecture 5f).

Groundwater recharge

Recharge estimates start from how much water falls on the landscape.

Glacier mass balance

Snowfall on a glacier is the “income” side of its mass balance.

Climate studies

PRISM also provides temperature, vapor pressure and elevation grids, so you can compare variables on the same grid.

Check your understanding

Answer in your own words first, then open the answer to check.

Q3 A PRISM cell has a value of 1,016 mm. What is it in inches?

1,016 ÷ 25.4 = 40 inches.

Q4 Your DEM has 10 m cells and PRISM has 800 m cells. Which pre-processing step deals with this, and what does it not do?

Resample. It matches the cell size so the rasters overlay cell for cell, but it does not add detail: the precipitation pattern is still only as fine as the 800 m source.

Q5 Put the four steps in a sensible order for a watershed study.

Project Raster → Extract by Mask (clip to the watershed) → Resample to match your other rasters → convert units (mm to inches) with Raster Calculator.
