Part 1

Why interpolate to a raster?

From scattered sample points to a continuous surface, and the idea that makes it possible

From points to a continuous surface

Key idea

Interpolation predicts values for every cell of a continuous raster from a limited number of sample points.

We rarely have data everywhere. We have scattered measurements: rain gauges, monitoring wells, elevation points.

A raster is a grid of cells, each with a value. Interpolation fills in the cells between the samples.

What can we interpolate?

Any value measured at geographic points, for example elevation, rainfall or depth to groundwater.

Read the figure

Cells near the point labelled 30 get high values; cells near 14 get low ones. The surface is our best estimate, not a measurement.

Lecture notes

In this lecture, we’re getting into one of the core ideas in GIS and hydrology: how we take a limited number of measured points and turn them into a continuous surface. In real-world applications, we rarely have data everywhere. Instead, we have scattered measurements like rain gauges, monitoring wells, or elevation points. Interpolation is the process that lets us estimate what’s happening in between those points. The end result is usually a raster, which gives us a complete spatial picture.

The main reason we interpolate is because we want to predict values where we don’t have measurements. In hydrology, this is extremely common. We might have rainfall data at a few stations, but we need a full surface to understand watershed processes. The same goes for elevation, groundwater levels, or temperature. Interpolation allows us to build a complete spatial dataset from incomplete observations.

Tobler’s law

Key idea

Tobler’s law: things that are close together tend to have similar characteristics. Spatially distributed objects are spatially correlated.

This is the foundation of almost every interpolation method.

When we estimate a value at a location, we assume nearby points are more relevant than distant ones.

Read the figure

The points on the left become the surface on the right. Areas between close, similar points get similar colors.

Watch for this idea in every method that follows: IDW uses it directly, and kriging measures it from the data.

Lecture notes

A key concept behind interpolation is Tobler’s Law, which says that things that are close together tend to be more similar than things that are far apart. This is the foundation for almost every interpolation method. When we estimate a value at a location, we assume nearby points are more relevant than distant ones. This idea shows up in all the methods we’ll talk about today.

Four interpolation methods in Spatial Analyst

Key idea

ArcGIS Spatial Analyst (or 3D Analyst) has four main interpolation methods. Think of them as a spectrum from simple to statistical.

1. IDW

Inverse distance weighting. A weighted average of nearby points; closer points count more.

Simple and distance-based.

2. Spline

Fits a smooth surface through every point, like a flexible sheet.

Focuses on smoothness.

3. Topo to Raster

Builds hydrologically correct elevation models from points, contours and streams.

Enforces physical realism.

4. Kriging

A geostatistical method that models spatial correlation and reports uncertainty.

Uses statistics.

Lecture notes

When we say “interpolation with Spatial Analyst,” we’re referring to tools in ArcGIS that automate this process. These tools allow us to generate raster datasets where every cell has a predicted value, even if we didn’t directly measure it there.

A good way to think about these methods is along a spectrum: IDW is simple and distance-based; Spline focuses on smoothness; Topo to Raster enforces physical realism (especially for terrain); Kriging uses statistical modeling of spatial relationships. Part of the goal today is helping you understand when each method makes sense.

Q1 You have 12 rain gauges in a watershed. Why would you interpolate them to a raster instead of just using the 12 values?

Watershed analysis needs a value everywhere: every cell of the watershed. Interpolation estimates the rainfall between the gauges so you can map it and compute totals.

Part 2

Inverse distance weighting (IDW)

A weighted average of nearby points, and the settings that shape it

How IDW works

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

IDW estimates each cell by averaging the sample points in its neighborhood. The closer a point is to the cell, the more weight it has.

  1. The tool looks at sample points within a neighborhood of the cell
  2. It calculates a weighted average of those points
  3. Weights depend on distance: closer points contribute more

This is where “inverse distance” comes from: influence decreases as distance increases. A power setting controls how fast.

Read the figure

The yellow circle is the neighborhood. The center point is where we estimate a value; the red points inside the circle each contribute, the closest ones most.

A local method

Each prediction depends mainly on nearby data. IDW does not consider broader patterns beyond the neighborhood.

Lecture notes

At its core, IDW (Inverse Distance Weighted) is based on a very intuitive idea: when we estimate a value at a location, we take an average of nearby sample points. But it’s not just a simple average. Each point is weighted based on how far away it is.

When we estimate the value of a raster cell, the software looks at surrounding sample points within a defined neighborhood. It calculates a weighted average of those points. The weights are based on distance, so closer points contribute more to the estimate than points farther away.

This is where the term “inverse distance” comes from. The influence of a point decreases as distance increases. Mathematically, this is often controlled by a power function, but conceptually, just remember: closer equals more important.

In the diagram, the yellow circle represents the neighborhood being considered, the center point is where we’re estimating a value, and the surrounding sample points each contribute to that estimate. Points closest to the center have the largest weights.

This leads to an important characteristic of IDW: it is a local interpolation method. Each prediction is mainly influenced by nearby data, and it does not consider broader spatial patterns beyond that neighborhood.

Each point has a local influence

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Every input point has a local zone of influence that fades with distance.

IDW weights points closer to the cell more than those farther away.

Each point “pulls” the surface toward its value, but only strongly within its neighborhood.

The result reflects local variation rather than broad regional trends.

Watch out

IDW can produce “bullseye” patterns: rings of high or low values around individual data points.

The settings in the dialog

The next slides walk through search radius, power and barriers.

Lecture notes

Each point only influences its surrounding area, not the whole map, and that influence decreases as distance increases. In IDW, every data point has a “zone of influence,” and that influence fades the farther you move away. So when estimating a value, nearby points matter more, and distant ones matter less. This creates a surface that reflects local variation rather than broad regional trends.

A good way to think about it is: each point “pulls” the surface toward its value, but only strongly within its neighborhood. IDW works well when you don’t want to assume complex spatial patterns, but it can produce “bullseye” patterns around data points if not used carefully.

Search radius: variable or fixed

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

The search radius decides which points are averaged. Variable (the default) lets the data decide; fixed uses the same circle everywhere.

Variable (default, usually best)

Uses a set number of points. In dense areas it searches a small area; in sparse areas it expands outward. It adapts to uneven data.

Fixed

Uses a circle of the same radius around every cell. In dense areas it may include too many points, in sparse areas too few.

Use fixed only when you have a good reason to control the spatial extent manually.

Lecture notes

Variable search radius: the number of points used changes depending on data density. With a variable search radius, the model adapts to your data. In areas with lots of points, it uses fewer nearby points. In sparse areas, it expands outward to include more points. This is the default and usually the best option because real-world data is rarely evenly distributed. It helps avoid gaps in sparse areas and overcrowding in dense areas.

Fixed radius: the same radius is used everywhere. Here, the model always looks within a fixed distance. This is simpler but less flexible. In dense areas, it may include too many points, and in sparse areas, it might not include enough. Use this only when you have a good reason to control the spatial extent manually.

Power

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Power controls how fast a point’s influence drops with distance. It decides whether the map looks patchy or smooth.

Higher power

  • Strong emphasis on nearer points
  • More local detail
  • A less smooth surface

Lower power

  • More influence from farther points
  • A smoother, more general surface
  • A more global approach

The default power in ArcGIS is 2. This is one of the most important IDW settings.

Lecture notes

Power controls how fast influence decreases with distance. Higher power puts strong emphasis on nearby points, giving more local detail and a less smooth surface. Lower power gives more influence to distant points, producing a smoother, more generalized surface. This is one of the most important IDW settings. It directly affects whether your map looks “patchy” or smooth.

Barriers

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

A barrier is a breakline that limits the search to sample points on the same side of the barrier.

Barriers act like walls: points on one side do not influence estimates on the other side.

Examples

  • Mountain ridges affecting rainfall
  • Rivers or faults affecting groundwater

Use a barrier when you know a physical feature should limit spatial influence.

In the dialog

Add the barrier as input barrier polyline features (optional).

Lecture notes

Barriers prevent influence across features. Barriers act like walls. Points on one side do not influence estimates on the other side. Examples include mountain ridges affecting rainfall, or rivers and faults affecting groundwater. This is useful when you know physical features should limit spatial influence.

Part 3

Spline

A smooth “rubber sheet” through every point, and the regularized and tension options

How spline works

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Spline fits a mathematical function that minimizes overall surface curvature, giving a smooth surface that passes exactly through the input points.

Spline is fundamentally different from IDW. Instead of averaging nearby points, it fits a continuous surface that bends as little as possible while still hitting every point.

The rubber sheet

Spline “rubber sheets” the points, or creates a Bezier curve through them. The curve in the figure passes through every orange point without sharp angles.

Assumption

The phenomenon changes smoothly across space. If your data has sharp jumps or abrupt boundaries, spline may not represent it well.

Lecture notes

Spline uses a mathematical function to create a smooth surface, minimizes overall surface curvature, and passes exactly through input data points. Spline is fundamentally different from IDW. Instead of just averaging nearby points, it fits a continuous surface that bends as little as possible while still hitting every data point exactly. This is why you’ll often hear the “rubber sheet” analogy—it’s like stretching a smooth surface over all your points.

One important implication is that spline assumes the phenomenon changes smoothly across space. If your data has sharp jumps or abrupt boundaries, spline may not represent it well.

When to use spline

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Spline is best for gently varying surfaces such as elevation, water table heights or pollution concentrations.

Good for

Variables that change gradually. Groundwater levels, for example, usually do not jump abruptly: they transition smoothly across space.

How it fits

Fits a minimum-curvature surface by applying a mathematical function to a specified number of nearest points, while passing through the sample points.

Watch out

Because the surface must pass through every point, spline is sensitive to outliers. One bad point can distort the surface, so data quality matters more than with IDW.

Lecture notes

Spline is best for gently varying surfaces. Common uses are elevation, water table height and pollution concentration. Spline is ideal when you expect gradual spatial change. For example, groundwater levels usually don’t jump abruptly—they transition smoothly across space.

However, spline forces the surface through all points exactly, which means it is sensitive to outliers, and bad data points can distort the surface significantly. So data quality matters more with spline than with IDW.

Regularized spline

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

The regularized option creates a smooth, gradually changing surface whose values may lie outside the sample data range.

Regularized emphasizes smoothness even more. The surface can overshoot the observed values: mathematically reasonable, but sometimes physically unrealistic.

Weight (regularized)

Defines the weight of the third derivatives of the surface in the curvature minimization. The higher the weight, the smoother the surface.

Typical values: 0, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1 and 0.5.

In practice

You may see predictions above your maximum or below your minimum. Always check outputs against known ranges.

Lecture notes

The regularized option produces very smooth, gradual surfaces, and can create values outside the observed data range. The regularized option emphasizes smoothness even more. It allows the surface to slightly overshoot the observed values, which can be mathematically reasonable but physically unrealistic. In practice, this means you may see predicted values higher than your max or lower than your min, so always check outputs against known ranges. This option is useful when smoothness is more important than strictly honoring data bounds.

The weight controls how smooth the surface is. Higher weight gives stronger smoothing, less local variation and a flatter surface. Lower weight gives more flexibility and more local detail. This parameter is essentially controlling how much the model penalizes curvature: how “tight” or “loose” that rubber sheet is.

Tension spline

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

The tension option controls the stiffness of the surface. It creates a less smooth surface whose values stay closer to the sample data range.

Tension is roughly the opposite of regularized smoothing: it tightens the sheet so it does not bend too much, reducing overshooting.

Weight (tension)

Defines the weight of tension. The higher the weight, the coarser (stiffer) the surface. Typical values: 0, 1, 5 and 10.

Use tension when

  • You want to avoid unrealistic peaks or dips
  • You expect sharper gradients in the data

Regularized → smoother, more flowing. Tension → tighter, more controlled.

Lecture notes

Tension controls stiffness of the surface and produces less smooth, more constrained surfaces. Tension is kind of the opposite of regularized smoothing. It tightens the surface so it doesn’t bend too much. This keeps values closer to the observed data and reduces overshooting. Use tension when you want to avoid unrealistic peaks or dips, or you expect sharper gradients in the data.

Higher weight gives a stiffer, less smooth surface. Increasing tension weight makes the surface resist bending. That results in a coarser surface with more localized variation. So compared to regularized: regularized is smoother and more flowing; tension is tighter and more controlled.

Number of points

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Number of points sets how many input points are used to calculate each interpolated cell. It controls how local or global the spline is.

More points

  • Each cell is influenced by more distant points
  • A smoother output surface
  • More global influence
  • Longer processing time

Fewer points

  • More local detail
  • More variability
  • Faster computation

This works like the neighborhood in IDW: it sets how local or global the interpolation is.

Lecture notes

Number of points controls how many neighbors are used for each estimate. More points means a smoother surface, more global influence and longer computation time. Fewer points means more local detail, more variability and faster computation. This is similar to IDW in that it controls how local vs global your interpolation is.

When you increase the number of neighboring points used in the calculation, each raster cell is influenced by a larger area. That spreads out the influence and reduces local variability, which makes the surface smoother.

Topo to Raster

Interpolating elevation that water can flow across correctly

Hydrologically correct DEMs

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Topo to Raster was designed to create hydrologically correct DEMs: surfaces where water flows the way it does on real terrain.

It interpolates a hydrologically correct surface from point, line and polygon data.

It is based on the ANUDEM program developed by Michael Hutchinson (1988, 1989).

Why it matters in hydrology

Unlike IDW and spline, this method is built for terrain. It does not just interpolate values: it makes sure the surface behaves correctly for water flow.

Small elevation errors can completely change flow direction, and with it your watershed boundaries and stream network.

Find it

Spatial Analyst Tools › Interpolation › Topo to Raster

Lecture notes

Topo to Raster is designed specifically for hydrologically correct DEMs and uses multiple data types (points, lines, polygons). Unlike IDW and spline, this method is built for terrain. It doesn’t just interpolate values—it ensures the surface behaves correctly for water flow. This is critical in hydrology because small elevation errors can completely change flow direction.

Topo to Raster in ArcGIS

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

The tool accepts several kinds of input at once and integrates them into one consistent surface.

Inputs you can combine

  • Contours
  • Stream networks
  • Elevation points
  • Lakes, sinks and boundaries

Read the dialog

Each row in Input feature data is one layer, with the field holding elevation (if any) and its type, such as PointElevation, Contour or Stream.

Then set the output surface raster, the cell size and the extent.

Lecture notes

This is the ArcGIS implementation of the method. This tool allows you to input contours, stream networks and elevation points. It then integrates all of these into a single consistent surface.

Why Topo to Raster works so well for terrain

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Topo to Raster combines the efficiency of local methods with the surface continuity of global methods, and enforces a connected drainage structure.

Works with contours

The only ArcGIS raster interpolator designed to work intelligently with contour inputs.

It is a discretized thin plate spline technique (Wahba, 1990).

Best of both worlds

Computational efficiency of local methods such as IDW

Without losing the surface continuity of global methods such as kriging and spline

Drainage built in

Constraints give a connected drainage structure and correct ridges and streams

Less data necessary

No editing needed to remove sinks

Lecture notes

Topo to Raster works well with contour data and balances local detail and global smoothness. It combines advantages of IDW (local influence) and spline/kriging (smoothness and continuity). This makes it very effective for DEM creation.

It enforces drainage structure, preserves ridges and streams, and removes sinks automatically. This is one of the biggest advantages. In many DEM workflows, you have to manually fix sinks. Topo to Raster largely avoids that step by building a hydrologically consistent surface from the start.

Part 5

Kriging

A geostatistical method that models spatial correlation and reports uncertainty

Deterministic vs. geostatistical methods

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

IDW and spline follow fixed rules. Kriging builds a statistical model of how the measured points relate to each other.

Deterministic: IDW and spline

Directly based on the surrounding measured values, or on specified mathematical formulas that set the smoothness of the surface.

No measure of how good the estimate is.

Geostatistical: kriging

Based on statistical relationships among the measured points (autocorrelation)

Produces a prediction surface by fitting a semivariogram

Provides a measure of the certainty or accuracy of the predictions

Lecture notes

IDW and Spline are deterministic; kriging is geostatistical (statistical-based). Up to now, IDW and spline were based on rules (distance or smoothness). Kriging is different because it builds a statistical model of spatial relationships in the data. This makes it more flexible, but also more complex. It uses spatial autocorrelation and produces both predictions and uncertainty. Kriging doesn’t just estimate values—it also tells you how confident those estimates are. That’s a big advantage in scientific and hydrologic applications where uncertainty matters.

Autocorrelation

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Autocorrelation: things that are close to one another are more alike than those farther away.

This is Tobler’s law again, but kriging quantifies it.

The model measures how similarity changes with distance in your data, and uses that to guide predictions.

The semivariogram

That measured relationship is the semivariogram. It describes how different two points tend to be as the distance between them grows.

Lecture notes

Nearby points are more similar than distant ones. This is similar to Tobler’s Law, but in kriging, this relationship is quantified mathematically. The model measures how similarity changes with distance and uses that to guide predictions.

Distance, direction and the kriging tool

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

The distance or direction between sample points reflects a spatial correlation that explains variation in the surface.

A mathematical function is applied to a specified number of points, or points within a specified radius, to determine the output value at each location.

Unlike IDW, kriging can capture

  • Directional trends (anisotropy), such as rainfall shaped by mountains
  • Spatial clustering

Read the dialog

Choose the kriging method (ordinary or universal), the semivariogram model (for example spherical), the search radius, and optionally an output variance of prediction raster.

Lecture notes

Kriging uses distance AND spatial structure, and can consider direction (anisotropy). Unlike IDW, which only uses distance, kriging can account for patterns like directional trends (e.g., rainfall influenced by mountains) and spatial clustering. This makes it much more realistic for environmental data.

The kriging equation

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Like IDW, kriging weights the surrounding measured values to predict an unmeasured location. The difference is how the weights are found.

Ẑ(s0) = Σ λi Z(si), i = 1 … N

  • Z(si): the measured value at the i-th location
  • λi: an unknown weight for the measured value at the i-th location
  • s0: the prediction location
  • N: the number of measured values

IDW vs. kriging weights

IDW: weights from distance only. Kriging: weights from the spatial correlation (semivariogram), so they are data-driven.

As locations get farther away, their values have little relationship with the prediction location.

Lecture notes

Kriging is a weighted sum of nearby values. It looks similar to IDW mathematically, but the key difference is how the weights are determined: IDW is based only on distance; kriging is based on spatial correlation (the semivariogram). So the weights are smarter and data-driven.

Influence still decreases with distance. Even though kriging is more advanced, distance still plays a role. Points farther away generally contribute less, but the rate of that decrease is controlled by the statistical model rather than a fixed formula.

Kriging uses the data twice

  1. IDW
  2. Spline
  3. Topo to Raster
  4. Kriging
Key idea

Kriging uses the data twice: first to estimate the spatial autocorrelation, then to make the predictions.

  1. Build the semivariogram: how similarity changes with distance
  2. Use that model to estimate values across the raster

Two outputs

Left: the interpolated surface raster, the predictions you usually care about.

Right: the prediction variance (uncertainty). It is lowest near the samples (dark) and grows away from them.

Kriging has many types: ordinary (most common), universal and indicator. See ArcGIS Help for more.

Lecture notes

Kriging uses data twice: to model spatial correlation and to make predictions. Step 1 is building the semivariogram, which describes how similarity changes with distance. Step 2 uses that model to estimate values across the raster. This two-step process is what makes kriging more powerful than simpler methods.

The interpolated surface raster is the main output you typically care about. It shows how the variable changes continuously across space based on the model. In kriging workflows, you often generate multiple outputs: predicted values and prediction error or uncertainty. The grayscale map typically represents uncertainty or prediction variance.

Kriging is not just one method. There are different types: ordinary kriging (most common), universal kriging and indicator kriging. ArcGIS has additional settings, but for now, focus on understanding the concept rather than all the variations.

Other less common methods
